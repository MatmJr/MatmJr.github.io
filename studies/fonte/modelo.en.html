Self-organized criticality in Python

The sandpile

Grains fall one by one onto a board. When a cell collects four, it collapses and passes one grain to each neighbour, which may collapse too.

Over time the system tunes itself into a state where a single grain can do nothing at all or bring down half the board. This guide explains the theory behind that and builds, step by step, an animation that pauses when the risk of a catastrophic avalanche gets high and shows where it could start.

Part I

Theory

What self-organized criticality is, how the model is defined, and what can be measured and predicted in it. The figures quoted here were measured with this guide's own code.

1Self-organized criticality

Earthquakes, forest fires, snow avalanches, mass extinctions and bursts of activity in the brain have something in common: they produce events of every size. There are many small ones, some medium ones and, every now and then, a giant one. There is no typical size. When you count how many events of each size s happened, the result follows a power law:

N(s) ∝ s−τOn a log-log plot this is a straight line with slope −τ. The Gutenberg–Richter law for earthquakes is of this kind.

In physics, power laws show up at the critical points of phase transitions, such as water at its critical point or a magnet at the Curie temperature. There, however, someone has to tune the temperature precisely. Nobody tunes anything in nature, and yet power laws are everywhere.

In 1987, Per Bak, Chao Tang and Kurt Wiesenfeld proposed an explanation: some systems reach the critical point on their own and stay there. They called this self-organized criticality (SOC). The example they used is the one in this guide, a sandpile. Four ingredients appear in every system of this kind:

  • Slow drivingEnergy comes in gradually, one grain at a time.
  • Local thresholdNothing happens until a cell goes over a limit.
  • Fast relaxationA cell over the limit discharges into its neighbours, and the avalanche ends before the next grain.
  • DissipationSomething leaves the system. Here, the grains that fall off the edge. Without that, the pile would grow forever.

2The Bak–Tang–Wiesenfeld model

Take a square L × L lattice. Each site x = (i, j) holds an integer z(x) ≥ 0, its height, which is the number of grains. The neighbours of x are the four sites above, below, left and right. The critical threshold is zc = 4, the number of neighbours. The dynamics has three rules:

  1. Driving. Pick x with uniform probability 1/L² and set z(x) ← z(x) + 1.
  2. Toppling. If some site y has z(y) ≥ zc, it topples: z(y) ← z(y) − 4, and every neighbour w inside the lattice gets one grain, z(w) ← z(w) + 1. A grain headed for a neighbour outside the lattice is lost.
  3. Relaxation. Repeat rule 2 until no site has z ≥ zc. Only then go back to rule 1.

Rule 3 is the separation of time scales: driving is infinitely slow compared with an avalanche. A board with no unstable sites is called stable, and every height in it is 0, 1, 2 or 3.

In matrix notation, toppling y means subtracting a column of the matrix Δ:

z ← z − Δ ey,   with Δyy = 4  and  Δyw = −1 for each neighbour wΔ is the discrete Laplacian of the lattice, and ey is the vector with 1 at y and 0 elsewhere.

In the bulk, a toppling conserves grains: the cell loses 4 and its neighbours gain 4. Only at the edge does the total go down. That dissipation is what lets the system settle into an equilibrium.

3Avalanches: size, area and duration

The chain of topplings set off by one grain is an avalanche. Three measures describe each one:

Size s
Total number of topplings. A cell can topple more than once in the same avalanche.
Area a
Number of distinct cells that toppled. Always a ≤ s.
Duration T
Number of waves. In a wave, every unstable cell topples at the same time. Cells made unstable by them topple in the next wave.

The animation shows each avalanche wave by wave: bright red for cells toppling now and light red for cells that have already toppled.

4The critical state

Starting from an empty board, almost no grains leave through the edge. The mean density ⟨z⟩ climbs in a straight line and avalanches are small. This is the transient. Then the density stops climbing and starts to fluctuate around a plateau. From then on the system is in the critical state and stays there, without anyone tuning anything. On a 30 × 30 board, over 50,000 grains, we measured:

~2,100grains until the end of the transient
2.08grains per cell on the plateau (infinite lattice: ≈ 2.125)
42.5%of grains set off some avalanche
84.5topplings per avalanche, on average

The first giant avalanche usually shows up right near the end of the transient, between grains 1,900 and 2,100: that is when the system reaches the critical state. After that, the size distribution follows a power law up to a cutoff:

P(s) ∼ s−τ,   for 1 ≪ s ≪ sc(L)

The cutoff sc grows with the board size L. Comparing L = 16, 32 and 64 and watching the cutoff move is the classic way to confirm criticality (finite-size scaling). On the 30 × 30 board we measured τ ≈ 1.05 to 1.07. On large lattices, estimates for the 2D BTW model fall around 1.1 to 1.3, and the exact value is still debated. Small boards tend to underestimate the exponent.

When building the histogram, use logarithmic bins. With linear bins the tail gets very few events per bin and turns into noise.

5The abelian property

In 1990, Deepak Dhar showed that the BTW model has a strong property: the final state does not depend on the order in which unstable cells topple. Neither does the number of times each cell topples. Writing ax for the operation "add a grain at x and relax", we have

ax ay = ay axThat is why the model is also called the abelian sandpile.

The intuition is simple. Toppling a cell only adds grains to its neighbours, and that never prevents another unstable cell from toppling. Any valid order ends up in the same place.

In the code

Two parts of the project rely on this. RegraBTW.ondas topples all unstable cells together, in waves; we compared it with the sequential version over 8,000 grains and the final states and sizes were identical. The risk monitor reuses the shared part of the avalanches of a whole cluster (section 7).

6Critical is not chaotic

It is tempting to call the avalanche chaotic, but the technical term does not apply. In a chaotic system, a tiny difference grows exponentially until it takes over everything: the butterfly effect. The sandpile is deterministic, given the board and where the grain lands, but a small difference does not get amplified.

To test this, we took two identical 30 × 30 boards, except for one extra grain in one cell, and dropped the same sequence of 20,000 grains on both:

The difference goes up and down, and in test 3 it shrinks back to a single cell. For about 90% of the grains, the avalanche had exactly the same size on both boards.

What does exist is sensitivity to the trigger point: in 119 of 870 pairs of neighbouring cells, a grain on one causes at most 5 topplings and on the other at least 100. The board sits at the threshold, full of regions about to collapse, and the unpredictability comes from that structure combined with the random grain.

7Can it be predicted? The risk of catastrophe

Pick a size s* from which an avalanche counts as a catastrophe. For a stable board z, let sx(z) be the size of the avalanche that would happen if the next grain landed on x. Since the dynamics is deterministic, this number can be computed in advance by simulating on a hypothetical board. The danger zone and the risk are:

Z(z) = { x : sx(z) ≥ s* }     R(z) = |Z(z)| / L²

With uniform drops, R(z) is exactly the probability that the next grain causes a catastrophe. The animation computes R before every grain, paints Z blue and pauses when R goes over a limit.

The cluster lemma

Call a cluster a connected set C of cells with z = 3, that is, one grain away from toppling. If the grain lands on c ∈ C, every cell of C topples at least once.

The proof is short. Cell c reaches 4 and topples. Each neighbour of c that belongs to C gets a grain, reaches 4 and topples as well. Repeating the argument along any path inside C, they all topple.

The shortcut

Thanks to the abelian property, you can topple the whole of C first and only then carry on. For any c ∈ C:

sc = |C| + SC + rc

SC is the number of topplings needed to stabilize the board after toppling C once. It is the same for every cell of C. Only rc, what the extra grain still causes at the end, depends on c, and it is usually small. So one large simulation per cluster is enough, instead of one per cell. On the 30 × 30 board, an evaluation went from 83 ms to 19 ms with identical results, checked on 27,000 cells.

Does the forecast work?

We ran 12,000 grains on a 30 × 30 board, with a catastrophe defined as an avalanche of at least 550 topplings (about 1% of avalanches) and the alert at R ≥ 5%:

Forecast chanceGrainsForecast (mean)Happened
0%6,3660%0%
up to 2%1,2361.04%1.29%
2% to 5%1,1753.28%3.06%

The forecast chance matches the observed frequency. More than half of the catastrophes (55 of 107) happened with the alert on, and none happened when the forecast chance was zero. Even with the alert on, though, about 92% of grains land outside the blue zone. It is a risk forecast, like seismic hazard maps: it tells you where and how likely, but not when.

Where each SOLID principle comes in

S

Single responsibility. Each class has one reason to change: the board stores state, the rule topples, the playback sets the pace, the animation draws.

O

Open/closed. New behaviour comes in as a new class, such as RegraBTW(VIZINHANCA_8) or SorteioCentro(), without editing Simulacao.

L

Liskov substitution. TabuleiroHipotetico can stand in for Tabuleiro anywhere. The rule runs on it without knowing it is a hypothesis, and that is how the monitor simulates "what if the grain lands here?".

I

Interface segregation. Interfaces are small: Sorteador only has escolher; RegraTombamento has tombar and ondas.

D

Dependency inversion. Simulacao and MonitorDeRisco depend on the abstractions. The concrete pieces are created in main.py and passed in through the constructor.

Step 1The board

The L × L lattice from the model. Tabuleiro only stores how many grains are in each cell and knows nothing about drops or avalanches.

  • Posicao = tuple[int, int] is just an alias, to keep the annotations readable.
  • __getitem__ lets you write tabuleiro[(i, j)].
  • adicionar accepts a negative amount. That is how the rule will take 4 grains out of a cell.
  • posicoes() is a generator: it walks the cells without building a list.
  • como_matriz() returns a copy, so whoever draws cannot change the state by accident.

TabuleiroHipotetico is the piece that makes the risk monitor feasible. It reads from the original board and keeps only the differences in a dictionary. Simulating an avalanche on it costs in proportion to the avalanche, not to the board, and the original does not change. It overrides total and como_matriz so that it keeps behaving like a real Tabuleiro (the L in SOLID).

Step 3The toppling rule

Rules 2 and 3 of the model. The RegraTombamento interface declares two methods, and RegraBTW implements them:

  • tombar(tabuleiro, pos) is rule 2 for one cell: it removes critico grains and gives 1 to each neighbour on the board. It returns the cells that received a grain.
  • ondas(tabuleiro, candidatas) is rule 3. It is a generator: each yield hands out one wave and pauses the function until someone asks for the next. That is what lets the animation show one wave per frame.
  • candidatas says where to look for unstable cells at the start. After a grain is dropped, only the cell where it landed can have become unstable, so there is no need to scan the whole board.
  • Inside the loop, tocadas collects the cells that toppled and the ones that received a grain. Only they can have become unstable for the next wave.

The threshold critico is computed as the number of neighbours. That way no grain is created or destroyed in the bulk, as the model requires.

Step 4The simulation

Avalanche is a dataclass that stores the origin and the list of waves. The three measures from section 3 become properties: tamanho (size), area and duracao (duration).

Simulacao receives a ready-made board, drawer and rule through its constructor (the D in SOLID). It offers three operations: soltar_grao (rule 1), ondas (relax gradually, for the animation) and passo (drop and relax in one go).

The file also defines Observador (observer), used by the statistical monitoring that writes every event to a CSV file. The animation does not need it, but keeping it does no harm.

Step 5The clusters

The cluster lemma needs the connected regions of cells one grain away from toppling. aglomerados does a depth-first search with a stack: it starts from a critical cell that has not been visited yet and keeps adding critical neighbours until there are none left. The other functions in the file (density, fraction of critical cells and largest cluster) are indicators used by the statistical monitoring.

Step 6The risk monitor

Section 7 turned into code:

  • tamanho_se_cair(tabuleiro, pos) computes sx: it creates a TabuleiroHipotetico, adds a grain at pos and counts the rule's topplings. The real board does not change.
  • _tamanhos_no_aglomerado is the shortcut sc = |C| + SC + rc: it topples the cluster once on a shared hypothetical board (comum), stabilizes it, and only then computes what is left for each cell. The hypothetical board for the remainder is built on top of comum, a "what if" inside another.
  • avaliar puts it all together and returns a Risco with the map, the zone Z, the chance R and whether there is an alert.

Cells with fewer than 3 grains are left out of the map: a grain on them brings nothing down.

Step 7Wave-by-wave playback

Without this piece, the animation would be too fast for a person to follow. ReproducaoEmOndas decides how far the simulation moves on each frame and draws nothing, which is why it can be tested without a window. It switches between two states:

dropping grainsup to 20 per frame; checks the risk before each one

← the avalanche ends
avalancheone wave per frame

Before each grain, _avaliar_risco asks the monitor. If the alert has just started, the playback stops before dropping the grain and sets alerta_novo (new alert), which the animation uses to pause. While the alert lasts, it does not pause again. _estado_avaliado avoids recomputing the risk of the same board when you resume after a pause.

Choosing the two limits

tamanho_catastrofico defines what counts as a catastrophe, and chance_alerta the risk at which the animation pauses. The two have to fit together: if the average chance of catastrophe is already above the alert limit, the alert stays on almost all the time. On the 30 × 30 board:

tamanho_catastroficograins causing a catastrophewith chance_alerta = 0.05
5501.1%rare alerts, only in the most dangerous states (≈ 5 times the average)
1507.4%the average is already above the limit, so the alert stays on most of the time; raise chance_alerta to something like 0.2

For another board size, measure the size distribution first and pick tamanho_catastrofico near the 99th percentile. On the 20 × 20 board in the demo above, that value is about 240.

Part III

Explore

With the pieces kept apart, each experiment below changes one line of main.py or adds a new class.

One-line experiments

  • The fractal. Simulacao(Tabuleiro(101), SorteioCentro(), regra) drops every grain in the centre and draws the sandpile fractal. Pass monitor=None to the playback: the chance calculation assumes uniform drops and does not hold here.
  • Eight neighbours. RegraBTW(VIZINHANCA_8) includes the diagonals. The threshold becomes 8 automatically and the colour list already has eight shades.
  • Board size. Larger boards have larger avalanches and an exponent τ closer to the literature, but the risk monitor gets slower, because there are more cells to evaluate.

Exercises

  1. Store the duration T of each avalanche and plot its log-log histogram. Does it follow a power law too?
  2. Count how many times each cell toppled over 50,000 grains and draw that map. Why is the centre more active than the edges?
  3. Write a new RegraTombamento in which a cell loses 4 grains but only passes 3 on, losing one along the way. Without touching Simulacao, check whether the power law survives or a typical avalanche size appears.
  4. Change the Sorteador so that grains fall more often in one region. What happens to the danger zone? Does the chance calculation still hold?
